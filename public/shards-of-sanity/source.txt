@@<h3><b>Problem</b></h3>

As more and more aspects of software engineering are being automated away, a fear that such a tendency will make us forget its implementational details is bound to ensue. Some people say that such details are archaic remnants rather than pieces of wisdom which are relevant to core principles of engineering, yet a corner of our intellectual heart tells us that the ability to deal with old-fashioned techniques, however irrelevant they may appear to be in today's world, does still serve its unique role in the understanding of fundamentals. After all, we know that being able to do arithmetic by hand does contribute to one's comprehension of the soul of mathematics, although it is often looked down upon as a mere cookbook of obsolete skills, whose only job is to scratch the surface of the conceptual essence rather than penetrate it from a philosophical angle.

The major difficulty, though, arises from the fact that we are now significantly less motivated to exercise the old-school techniques of computer science. Programming just for the sake of not forgetting how to program, due to our inner voice which tells us that programming is not just a means to an end but also a fountain of myriad academic inspirations, is a direct source of frustration. And even when we try to keep our mental muscles in shape by "programming just for fun", we inevitably encounter the absence of joy which originates from the lack of its practical ends. One cannot simply enjoy developing a computer program by traditional means, when it is so painfully obvious that such a manual approach is objectively "useless" compared to the level of intellectual automation we are witnessing today, as well as what we fear to witness tomorrow.

Some may claim that what we are experiencing is just yet another shift in the scope of human reason, and that we ought to sacrifice some degree of our personal attachment to low-level mechanics (as we saw from the advent of general-purpose machines, high-level languages, and APIs) in order to be able to work on a higher level of abstraction and think in a broader context. Still, however, we cannot ignore the feeling that something is missing here because we know that, when it comes to understanding something deeply, the ability to stay vividly aware of the legacies of its full history is indispensable.

So, what shall we do to keep up with the latest technologies and embrace their merit, while making sure not to lose our grasp on the strengths of our past selves? Force-feeding the past knowledge down our throats is one way, yet we all know that it requires either a great deal of self-discipline (which is becoming increasingly hard to maintain, as the past knowledge is becoming more and more irrelevant to our current challenges), or an educational institution which pushes us forward despite the absence of our own willpower. The former is fragile, and the latter is not quite affordable unless you are a student and will stay as a student for the rest of your life.

The engine of human inspiration has lost its fuel, and contemporary intellectual shortcuts have replaced our substance of passion with a decoy of meaning. And it is now time to recognize this unforeseen existential dread, and search for new horizons of imagination through which we will be able to live in this brave new world without locking ourselves up in an old ivory tower.

@@<hr>
@@<h3><b>Objective</b></h3>

There is an ongoing fear that every type of human endeavor, whether it be a trade, an academic pursuit, or even a practice of moral decency such as empathy, will eventually be taken over by our AI overlords and their ever-growing capacity. And those of you who have been attentive to the latest breakthroughs in technology will share the feeling that such a fear is hardly unfounded. What was once considered an absurd work of science fiction yesterday is part of today's mundane reality, and we seem to have no other choice but to merely contemplate the impending doom.

It is an inner conviction of mine, however, that there must be a way out of this airtight chamber.

It is true that the old habit of programming is technically "outdated", and it is true that some of today's most powerful AI agents are so advanced, that they are capable of even maintaining a large codebase with a decent sense of architectural integrity in mind. So it is understandable that a natural response to this phenomenon would be, "Just learn how to use AI! There is no way you will write code better than they do."

But of course, many of us are vividly aware that "learning how to use AI" is way too superficial a subject to preoccupy one's mind with. Some professionals may respond to this sentiment by saying, "Oh, for sure! Knowing how to use AI is not sufficient, just like memorizing the syntax of a particular programming language has nothing to do with the essence of computer science. What you really need to learn are the core concepts of computer science which are abstract in nature, rather than means of implementation."

This is a fair call, yet we ought to also remind ourselves that one cannot study abstract concepts without first observing concrete examples and repeatedly dealing with them in real life.

Philosophy is the ultimate destiny of human freedom, and its fullest realization begins with the process of abstraction (for otherwise the so-called "philosophy" is prone to quickly degrade into an endless word play of sophists, backed up by a mere assortment of personal word associations). Yet, since the human mind does not possess the ability to perceive abstract ideas directly, we need a language through which we can indirectly refer to such ideas. In computer science, a programming language does represent such a means of reference (i.e. an interface to the process of abstraction), although the public is often quick to dismiss it as "just a tool". We do not need to memorize its grammar, but we do need to be familiar with its "language pattern" in order to thoroughly grasp the underlying metaphysics.

@@<hr>
@@<h3><b>Solution</b></h3>

Here is my idea. In order to overcome the apparent dilemma between the convenience offered by vibe coding and the benefit of mental exercise offered by programming on our own, we must establish a clear distinction between part of the codebase which will be actively maintained by AI, and part of the codebase which will be strictly human-reserved.

Let me explain what I mean by this. If you have been working on a codebase with the help of a powerful AI agent, you may have realized that the smarter the agent gets, the more of a bottleneck your direct engagement in that codebase turns out to be. AI-generated code used to be quite clumsy and prone to numerous errors, both from technical and aesthetic points of view, which rendered frequent human intervention necessary. Just as much as your AI assistant helped you with quick code generation and troubleshooting, you were forced to guide its partially broken line of reasoning by reviewing and adjusting its output with almost as much rigor as when you were writing at least the core logic by hand. Due to this balancing factor, you could still claim your irreplaceable role as a software engineer and proudly say, "AI can write a bunch of code, but humans are still needed to maintain its quality!"

I myself used to hold this sentiment, up until the middle of this year (July 2026). It was the release of Claude Opus 5 which seriously made me change my mind. Today's model (Claude Opus version 5 and onward) does not simply stop once it analyzes the user's prompt and then modifies the codebase in a way which fits what the user asked for. It also proceeds to guess (in a shockingly accurate manner) the hidden design intentions behind what was explicitly mentioned by the user, and even goes so far as to discover a list of alternative solutions as well as hidden pitfalls through which the program might fail to function in a way the user might have intended. And it does not stop here. It even takes measures to simplify the codebase by abstracting out redundant pieces of code into generic modules (Up until July 2026, I had to frequently instruct Claude to carry out this kind of refactoring; now I don't even have to), performs end-to-end tests (which it devised on its own) to verify that all the scenarios work as expected, and even learns from every test failure by memorizing what went wrong and what was the mistake from which such a failure originated. Can you believe it? It is adjusting its own decision-making habit (at least within the scope of the project) by directly observing the outcome of its own actions, just like a scientist would keep revising a hypothesis by conducting experiments in a laboratory and drawing conclusions from them. And it frequently reflects upon the structural complexity of the code it wrote, and manages to keep the level of tech debt fairly low by cleaning it up on its own. The depth of engineering insight which Claude has gained over the past few months is more than stunning.

At this point, therefore, the idea of co-authoring the same area of the codebase with an AI agent (for the sake of keeping human engineers in charge) seems to be losing its strength. What's the use of even looking at a piece of AI-authored code, if its quality is so undeniably high that any human endeavor to analyze and modify it in the hope of boosting its quality a bit further is not really worth the effort anymore? A pretty straightforward analogy can be found in craftsmanship. You may manually craft a wooden table that is endowed with delicate details which are unique to a human carpenter, to show off that your mastery in the field of carpentry is not fully replicable by a wood-carving machine. However, you will not be able to deny the cost-effectiveness of wooden tables which are mass-produced in a factory.

It is necessary, therefore, to wake up from the illusion that a human engineer can somehow work WITH an AI agent when it comes to writing and maintaining code. The moment you delegate a portion of your work to a competent agent, you are turning yourself into a manager who will eventually be expected to stop micromanaging and simply admit that trusting the work of an expert without knowing its details is inevitable.

Fortunately, however, this is not the end of the story. The fact that you have to become a manager in some aspects of engineering does not mean that you are required to be stuck in a management role without any chance to participate in software craftsmanship.

@@<h3><b>Solution Part 1: Human-Reserved Code</b></h3>

<000>

What we need is a clear separation of the human-reserved part from the rest of the codebase. And when it comes to the question of "Which part?", I would say that it depends on which subject you are primarily dealing with as an engineer. This is not a new concept; even way before the mainstream debut of AI-driven coding tools, people were developing software products using ready-made libraries for the implementation of UI frameworks, real-time graphics, physics engines, financial transactions, audio, in-app advertisements, and countless other pieces of logic which could be decoupled from the application's core mechanism in a fairly organized way. Delegating portions of one's development work to third parties has been a common industrial practice for decades, and it has been common for generalist developers to ridicule anyone who would build rudimentary modules from scratch by saying, "Don't reinvent the wheel!"

It is not so absurd an idea, therefore, for an engineer to work on a fraction of a large-scale software project rather than trying to be actively engaged in every single piece of it. As long as there is a clear boundary between part of the work which is one's own and part of the work which can be attributed to external entities, you will be able to claim your own world of intellectual endeavors in which you will preserve a room for sanity, reason, and growth.

You can turn this concept into reality by making a dedicated place within your codebase (e.g. a separate source file directory) in which you will be writing code entirely on your own, without any chance of interference by an AI agent (Enforcing this rule as part of the instruction for your AI agent will be a good starting point). Any piece of code you write within this reserved area, then, will be something which you will be able to confidently claim as your own, as well as something you will be able to manipulate with ease because no intermediary step during your course of formulating it would be skipped due to the help of AI.

Merely allocating a portion of the codebase and treating it as some kind of "human reservation", however, is not sufficient because it does not provide us with a clear reason why this thing is supposed to be treated as an exception and not be included as part of the automated workflow. Because of this, it is important for you to assign an indispensable yet independent role to your manually written code.

@@<hr>
@@<h3><b>Solution Part 2: Separation of the Pure Part</b></h3>

The key is to recognize the "pure side" of your project, which is functionally distinct yet still capable of working with the rest of the codebase. A quick example of this can be found in the game called "The Sims".

For those of you who do not know, The Sims is a life simulation game where you can create virtual people called "sims" and make them live inside virtual houses (It is like playing with a dollhouse, but the dolls are smart enough to make their own decisions and move on their own).

One can easily determine the "pure side" of The Sims; it is the collection of the game's core mechanics, such as the behavioral characteristics of each individual sim (e.g. how a motive triggers a sequence of actions, or how a personality trait determines the rate at which a motive gets aroused), house construction rules, item placement logic, distribution of random good/bad luck, and so on.

So, for instance, you may come up with your own human-reserved code which illustrates the way a sim updates its internal state, such as:

#$
// File Location: [src/humanReserved/.../sim.ts]

export default class Sim
{
    private environment: Environment;
    private location: Location;
    private needs: Need[];
    private tasks: Queue<SimTask>;
    
    tick(dt: number): void
    {
        this.inspectEnvironment(this.environment, this.location);
        this.updateNeeds(dt);
        this.tryStartNextTask(dt);
    }
    // ...
}
#$

Meanwhile, you can let your AI agent import your module into the code it has generated for the purpose of graphical rendering and other non-core parts of the game, like the one shown below:

#$
// File Location: [src/aiReserved/.../simGameObject.ts]

import Sim from "(...)/humanReserved/(...)/sim.ts";

export default class SimGameObject extends GameObject
{
    // The "sim" object is the purely logical side of the game character,
    // written entirely by a human engineer.
    private sim: Sim;

    // "components" are the GameObject's composable building blocks for specific
    // implementation details, such as "Mesh", "Animator", "AudioPlayer", etc.
    private components: Map<string, GameObjectComponent>;

    assignSim(sim: Sim): void
    {
        this.sim = sim;
    }
    update(dt: number): void
    {
        for (const component of this.components.values())
            component.update(this.sim, dt);
    }
    // ...
}
#$

In this example, the "Sim" class shown above can be considered pure because it does not depend on a particular tech stack; you may as well port it into any game engine (such as Unity or Unreal) and make it run inside the game without any major difficulty, given that it is properly integrated. The UI, graphics pipeline, asset/config management tools, and other platform-specific elements can be managed separately from the very mechanics of "Sim", which means it is possible to let the AI handle them without exposing you to the risk of losing control over the core logic.

@@<h3><b>Beyond Human Exceptionalism</b></h3>

There is one remaining question which might be bugging you still. And that is, "Is there any objective reason why I shouldn't just delegate the full process of engineering, including the formulation of the program's core logic, to my AI agent?"

What I really hate to admit is that there seems to be no decisive evidence to dismiss such a doubt. If your AI agent is capable of building websites, management tools, editors, interactive physics simulations, and countless other types of applications, on what basis shall we insist that the very "soul" of the software will never be replicated by LLMs? Why, because they "cannot possibly be conscious"? Or, because they "cannot truly understand human creativity"? Or perhaps, because they "only know how to imitate human intelligence without actually being intelligent"?

It is indeed comforting to come up with words that are vague in meaning (such as "consciousness", "creativity", or "intelligence"), and use them to make up an argument which cannot be disproven because there are multiple ways of interpreting it. But if you are a rational thinker, such a rash sort of conclusion will not leave you satisfied.

Instead of relying on the very ambiguity of our natural language (i.e. English) to justify wishful thinking, therefore, we must acknowledge the chance of facing even the most inhuman moments of disillusionment within our lifetime. We cannot entirely rule out the possibility that machines may eventually be able to reproduce even the subtlest corner of human glory.

Yet, there is one thing which will stay valid no matter what happens. It is the particularity of your very presence as a software engineer, right among your neighbors in a local community, where your own work has its special meaning for those who are using your software regardless of how insignificant it is measured to be from a holistic viewpoint. In the heart of your audience who have been acquainted more intimately with your own product than anything else in the world, familiarity triumphs over the objectivity of value.

@@<h3><b>A Starting Ground</b></h3>

In our introductory volume (i.e. volume 1), I suggested separating out a portion of the codebase and treating it as a special place reserved for human engineers. And in order to assign an indispensable role to it, I hinted at the importance of equipping the human-reserved code with a human-specific intention which is not quite parallel to that of an AI agent at least within the scope of a locality of human beings.

A major problem is that defining the volitional elements of a software product seems to be a monumental task which encompasses a rich spectrum of interdisciplinary subjects, such as arts, humanities, and philosophy. And because of this broadness in context, it is easy for an engineer to be perplexed by the apparent lack of connection between daily software engineering practices (e.g. technical design, documentation, programming) and ideas in the liberal arts.

What we ought to do foremost, then, is to discover such a connection - i.e. a layer of translation between the underlying morals of human nature and its rational symbolism. And in order to do this, we need to establish a habit of expressing seemingly irrational concepts (e.g. emotions, personalities, aesthetics) in terms of mathematical ideals.

@@<h3><b>A Point of Compromise</b></h3>

As the handling of domain-specific knowledge is being overtaken by LLMs, it seems natural to expect engineers to look for inspirations in general topics which do not quite fit inside the rationality of classical software development. Due to this apparent conflict between two schools of thought, many people tend to cling to either of the two extreme viewpoints - the pessimism of a Luddite and the optimism of a tech enthusiast. The former believes that computer science will slip out of our hands unless we exclude AI from our work entirely, and the latter believes that this new industrial revolution is simply a prelude to a new paradigm in computer science.

Such a dichotomy, however, ought to be taken as a lazy means of bypassing the most urgent question, which is: "What do we need to do now?" What we should be demanding is a practical solution, rather than an ideal which applies to only a select few individuals. Saying that you must isolate yourself from the AI-infested world cannot be an answer unless you are planning to become a hermit. Suppressing the usage of AI by means of regulation will only work as a temporary remedy because, as long as not everyone is subject to regulation, there will always be a risk of being run over by external competitors. Saying that you must simply follow the latest trend in technology, on the other hand, only lets charlatans get richer by selling buzzwords and generic catchphrases, while keeping the audience uninformed and thus easily manipulable.

It is necessary, therefore, to take a step back from such single-minded generalities and look for a pragmatic method of overcoming our present difficulties. The most pressing problem we need to solve in the context of software development is that of achieving a point of compromise between computer science and general humanities for the sake of keeping our spirit of computational thinking alive while also staying flexible enough to handle problems in multifaceted ways.

@@<hr>
@@<h3><b>Computational Representation of a Narrative</b></h3>

On a holistic level, both computer science and humanities belong to the same vein; it is the sense of human intentionality from which both of them originate. The highest challenge lies in the problem of coming up with a language which speaks both of these disciplines at once.

One major clue can be found in the word "narrative". It is the concept which plays a central role in the design of user experience (UI/UX), and is something which nearly everyone, who is in charge of developing and maintaining the project, should be familiar with regardless of one's field of expertise. Through the user's story, the product builds its own irreplaceable points of attraction apart from its functional side.

Yet, the very idea of branding a product by means of storytelling does not translate easily into design details. As a result, an inexplicable gap between "people who understand the customer" and "people who know how to make things work" is bound to arise, as long as we are content with the notion of a narrative as a mere queue of case-by-case needs.

In order to resolve this lack of structural integrity, we need to figure out a way to model the product's narrative from a rational standpoint; this way, we will be able to treat it as part of a single coherent system. This is the core philosophy which will empower engineers to directly control the full course of user interaction.

@@<hr>
@@<h3><b>Elements of a Narrative</b></h3>

When it comes to treating a narrative systematically, it is requisite to decompose it into elements which are endowed with a sense of mathematical purity.

One may insist that a narrative is supposed to be meaningful in ways which cannot possibly be expressed in terms of quantitative definitions, and that any attempt to do so will eventually result in a false replica of the subtlety of human nature. Such an argument may yield a decisive conclusion to those who do not understand the virtue of abstraction and are quick to discredit a general statement under the presence of "exceptional cases"; but if we are to use this kind of sentiment as an excuse to dismiss the merit of an attempt to provide a rational explanation for something which feels irrational, there will equally be no reason to employ the language of mathematics in the research of complex human activities such as economics, politics, and experimental psychology.

The matter of how far we can stretch our rationality, though, is not of the highest priority right now. The most important question we need to ask at this point is, "What are the basic building blocks out of which a narrative is composed, which are so pure in their characteristics that they can be part of a computational process?" This is the crux of the problem, and it is what I am going to answer now.

@@<hr>
@@<h3><b>Identities</b></h3>

You and I probably have different ideas when it comes to the question of what a narrative really is and what its ultimate goals are. Still, however, we can both agree on the point that every topic we are ever going to discuss needs to have its own definitions. By defining things, we enchant words with their special meanings, each of which can be summoned as a solid image in our architecture of imagination.

The most primitive type of definition is that of identity. In our everyday lives, we often say that something "is" something else, or that something "is not" something else. This reminds us of the nature of dualism in our logic.

In Alice's Adventures in Wonderland, we have a number of characters and they all possess their own identities. For example, Alice is a human and Dinah is a cat.

#$
Alice is a human.
Dinah is a cat.
#$

<001>

In a programming language, Alice and Dinah are both objects while human and cat are classes (categories) of objects. Human is a class to which Alice belongs, which implies that Alice is an instance of human. Cat is a class to which Dinah belongs, which implies that Dinah is an instance of cat.

#$
class Human {}
class Cat {}

const alice = new Human();
const dinah = new Cat();
#$

If we are to stretch this concept further into chains of indirect definitions, we will eventually end up seeing that identities which differ on a surface level may ultimately originate from their common ancestor. For example, we know that Alice is a human and Dinah is a cat, but do we know what we mean by "human" and what we mean by "cat"? What exactly is a human anyways? And what exactly is a cat?

If we put aside the comprehensive library of details in evolutionary biology and the full taxonomy of species which it yields, we may proceed to model our worldview in a rather simplified manner by conceiving yet another word which could be accused of being "way too general" but is not nonsensical either. In general, both humans and cats may be classified as animals. Alice is a human and Dinah is a cat, yet they are both animals because a human is an animal and a cat is an animal too.

#$
Alice is a human.
Dinah is a cat.
A human is an animal.
A cat is an animal.
#$

<002>

And it happens that animal is a class, too, since it functions as a means of classification just like human or cat does. Unlike human or cat, however, animal should be considered more abstract because the identification of Alice and Dinah as "animals" is more indirect than those involving the words "human" and "cat".

An animal is a more abstract representation of a human, as well as a more abstract representation of a cat. As a result, we say that a human or cat is a more concrete case of an animal, whereas an animal is a form of generalization whose definition applies to both of them without introducing any logical absurdity.

#$
class Animal {}
class Human extends Animal {}
class Cat extends Animal {}

const alice = new Human();
const dinah = new Cat();
#$

@@<h3><b>Relations</b></h3>

Oh, but guess what? Dinah is not just one of those stray cats on the street; she is Alice's cat. Alice owns her, so it makes sense for us to say that Alice has Dinah.

This is a different sort of relation from the ones which involved the word "is". Indeed, Alice is not Dinah and Dinah is not Alice; they are separate beings. Yet they are still bound together by their mutual bond, which is associated with the notion of ownership and dependence.

#$
Alice is a human.
Dinah is a cat.
Alice has Dinah.
#$

<003>

Another way of putting it would be that Alice owns Dinah and Dinah is owned by Alice. This kind of expression often manifests itself in the form of member variables in class definitions, which can be quite specific in meaning and be capable of changing their states over time. For instance, we may generally state that a human is able to own an animal as a pet, and that a cat is able to be owned by a human.

#$
class Human extends Animal
{
    pet: Animal;
}
class Cat extends Animal
{
    owner: Human;
}
#$

What we are seeing is a pair of references, one of them being held by a human and the other one being held by a cat. The presence of these fields called "pet" and "owner", by means of their semantic implications, suggests that a human is capable of having any animal as a pet, as well as that a cat is capable of having any human as its owner.

This naturally leads us to the idea that Alice, a human being, must be able to have her own pet. And guess what her pet's name is? Dinah! Meanwhile, Dinah is a cat, so she must be able to have an owner, who happens to be Alice.

We can establish such a pair of relations by saying "Alice's pet is Dinah, and Dinah's owner is Alice", which is syntactically more verbose than a general statement such as "Alice has Dinah" but retains its merit as a richer piece of illustration.

#$
const alice = new Human();
const dinah = new Cat();

alice.pet = dinah;
dinah.owner = alice;
#$

<004>

@@<hr>
@@<h3><b>Predicates</b></h3>

We know that Alice has Dinah as her pet. Yet, what might be bugging us at this point is that the way we conclude that Alice "has" Dinah, based on the fact that Dinah is Alice's pet and Alice is Dinah's owner, feels rather too implicit. Therefore, it is reasonable to insert an explicit piece of logic (member function) which tells us that any human "has" the pet he/she owns.

#$
class Human extends Animal
{
    pet: Animal;

    has(animal: Animal): boolean
    {
        return this.pet == animal;
    }
}
#$

This is a convenient way of establishing means of simplification - i.e. a facade of encapsulation which hides details behind it and only exhibits the purest essence of what needs to be conveyed. While the statement "Alice's pet is Dinah and Dinah's owner is Alice" is technically correct, it appears to be rather verbose and tainted with unnecessary crumbs of information. Replacing such an exhaustive depiction with a minimal expression (e.g. "Alice has Dinah") is how we optimize our methods of communication.

What is demonstrated below, for instance, proves the elegance of using a single predicate called "has" to state the relation of ownership between Alice and Dinah without explicitly mentioning the word "pet" or "owner" upfront.

#$
const alice = new Human();
const dinah = new Cat();

alice.pet = dinah;
dinah.owner = alice;

console.log(alice.has(dinah)); // Output: "true"
#$

@@<hr>
@@<h3><b>Aggregates</b></h3>

Another question may arise by now. What if we also want to mention that Alice has both Dinah and Kitty as her cats? For sure, this will suggest that we must have yet another cat called Kitty besides Dinah, and that there must be some kind of parallel between the way Alice is related to Dinah and the way Alice is related to Kitty. Just like Dinah, Kitty is a cat and Alice owns her.

#$
Alice is a human.
Dinah is a cat.
Kitty is a cat.
Alice has Dinah.
Alice has Kitty.
#$

<005>

Such a multiplicity of information can be achieved by expanding our definition. Suppose that a human is able to have a multitude of pets, not just one. This will be equivalent to having an array of pets rather than just a single pet. The "has" predicate, then, will be responsible for telling us that the human "has" the given animal as long as at least one of the pets happens to be that animal.

#$
class Human extends Animal
{
    pets: Animal[] = [];

    has(animal: Animal): boolean
    {
        return this.pets.some(pet => pet == animal);
    }
}
#$

Since Alice is a human, we can now let Alice have multiple pets. If we are to say that Alice owns exactly two pets called Dinah and Kitty, the most accurate way of putting it will be that Alice's "pets" is an array of two pets; the first element of the array could be Dinah, while the second element of the array could be Kitty.

#$
const alice = new Human();
const dinah = new Cat();
const kitty = new Cat();

alice.pets = [dinah, kitty];
dinah.owner = alice;
kitty.owner = alice;

console.log(alice.has(dinah)); // Output: "true"
console.log(alice.has(kitty)); // Output: "true"
#$

<006>

@@<h3><b>Beyond States</b></h3>

Things which have been illustrated so far, however, are mere statements which tell us of things out of which the world is made and how they are related to each other. As you may have already noticed, this is hardly anything more than a static snapshot of what is going on in our surroundings, which does not reveal the prospect of how things ought to behave and what their ultimate destination is, as well as the moral implications of what we are wishing for and what we are trying to avoid.

The logical constructs which I have introduced in this volume, therefore, only fill up an incomplete picture of a narrative. In order for a story to unfold organically and in a manner which convinces our heart of motivation - that is, to come up with something valuable (i.e. an objective which the audience deems worthwhile to pay attention to), we need to appeal to what are called "passion", "emotion", as well as myriad other fragments of subjectivity which do not seem to be quite compatible with a framework of reason established by hard science.

To overcome this lack of compatibility, we must invent a whole new language which neither the most sentimental poet nor the purest mathematician has been consciously speaking so far. Such a language ought to (and must) wake us from the intellectual lethargy of this new dark age.

Being new, however, does not require alien words. We can keep using words which are already familiar to us. The real difference lies in the subtlety of their inner meaning, which is what I am going to demonstrate with clarity in the upcoming volumes.

@@<hr>
@@<h3><b>Elements of a Narrative</b></h3>

I have hitherto been discussing the basic elements out of which a story could be composed, which yields a logical basis for the product's storytelling and hence its overall narrative (which is also associated with the idea of branding).

As I have also mentioned, though, the illustrations I presented in the previous volume still miss the core piece of the puzzle which is requisite to tackle our main problem - i.e. that of developing a narrative. In order for your software project to have its own story to tell (i.e. have its own "spirit"), you need to build a sequence of events which are designed to serve their own objective. What would that be? This is a much deeper question than that of technical design, for what we are facing here pertains to the purpose of life itself, as well as the sense of motivation which springs from it.

The sheer complexity of the problem we are dealing with, though, ought not to prevent us from exploring the veiled horizon of possibilities which may remind us of the struggle of the pioneers in abstract arts against the tyranny of materialism in the early 20th century. What we need is a list of basic archetypes, designed to offer us the power to keep articulating the landscape of human wisdom without hitting a hard limit. Here, I will show you an example.

@@<hr>
@@<h3><b>Goal</b></h3>

There are moments when we feel the necessity to express not only the current state of affairs, but also how things are supposed to be in the future. We all know that Alice is a human and Dinah is a cat, but what if Alice wishes that her cat, Dinah, were a human instead? One's ideal does not always align with how things currently look; there are, and will always be, mismatches between one's hope and reality.

#$
Alice is a human.
Dinah is a cat.
Alice wishes Dinah is a human.
#$

<007>

(Note: Each pink sequence of arrows should be thought of as something that is imagined rather than something that is necessarily true.)

The current state and the ideal, of course, do not always misalign. Sometimes they happen to happily coincide, in which case one's wish could be said to have been already fulfilled. If there is a guy (a human being) called Bob and Alice wants Bob to be human, we would say that Alice's wish has already been achieved because Bob already is a human.

#$
Alice is a human.
Bob is a human.
Alice wishes Bob is a human.
#$

<008>

Why? The answer will turn out to be fairly straightforward if you inspect the diagram above. A separate pathway which traverses the words [Bob -> is -> human] already exists, apart from what Alice wants. That is, "Bob is a human" is an independent statement (i.e. fact) and not just a mere wish. Alice's wish is a pathway which traverses the words [Alice -> wishes -> Bob -> is -> human], and here we can clearly see that the [Bob -> is -> human] portion of her wish is perfectly aligned with an independently claimed fact, meaning that the goal-condition of her wish (i.e. "Bob is a human") is already true and therefore does not require any further action to make it even truer.

@@<hr>
@@<h3><b>Will</b></h3>

The idea which I have discussed so far illustrates the nature of an objective, which is typically expressed in terms of words such as "wish", "want", "desire", "hope", and so on. The very presence of an objective alone, however, does not suffice when it comes to the execution of one's plan to achieve it. A wish will remain a wish forever, as long as we just keep contemplating it without taking any action to turn it into reality.

A story will manage to tear apart its petty nutshell of self-reflection and pave the way to its own heroic journey, once it stops merely looking at a wish and starts doing something to get closer to it. This is when we need the protagonist's willpower to break the ice and actively be in charge of what he/she is doing - i.e. a set of voluntary actions to make one's dream come true.

Since transforming a cat into a human being is not a realistic goal (at least outside of the realm of magic), Alice's desire to turn Dinah into a bipedal mammal is probably not going to yield any fruitful result no matter what she chooses to do. For now, therefore, let us suppose that she just wants Dinah to be close to her.

#$
Alice wishes Dinah is with Alice.
#$

<009>

This is a much more realistic wish than what was said before. Unlike the transformation of one animal species into another (which biologists have found out to be rather difficult), simply transporting one animal to the vicinity of another is far more feasible and can be achieved by means of a sequence of definable actions.

Since Alice wants to be near Dinah, all she needs to do to achieve this goal is simply move herself to a location which is closer to where Dinah is. In other words, Alice just needs to "go to" Dinah in order to be with Dinah.

#$
Alice wishes Dinah is with Alice.
Alice will go to Dinah.
#$

<010>

Here, though, you might be feeling that something is a bit too vague. And the reason why this is the case is that the statement "Alice will go to Dinah" would not really make that much sense if Alice did not wish to be with Dinah. We know that Alice would choose to move toward Dinah IF she wanted to be closer to Dinah. If Alice wished to be as far away from Dinah as possible, the statement "Alice will go to Dinah" would be nonsensical because it suggests a voluntary action which contradicts Alice's intention. The aforementioned statement, therefore, cannot exist independently; its origin must depend on the presence of the necessary precondition which says, "Alice wishes Dinah is with Alice".

Our logical conclusion, then, will be: "Alice will go to Dinah IF Alice wishes Dinah is with Alice". But then, if we do presume that it is a known fact that Alice wants to be close to Dinah, our assertion can be in a more definite form such as: "Alice will go to Dinah BECAUSE Alice wishes Dinah is with Alice".

#$
Alice wishes Dinah is with Alice.
Alice will go to Dinah because Alice wishes Dinah is with Alice.
#$

<011>

@@<hr>
@@<h3><b>Reference</b></h3>

By now, you may also have realized that there is a great deal of redundancy here. When speaking, we are usually not so fond of repeating the same phrase over and over again as long as the context is already clear. It will be wiser, therefore, to avoid repetition by using a reference to an already asserted fact instead of restating it every time it is meant to be taken into consideration.

When we say "Alice wishes Dinah is with Alice" and then make a statement whose cause depends on it, it is pretty obvious to us that we don't really have to say the preceding statement (i.e. "Alice wishes Dinah is with Alice") all over again; instead, we may as well just use the word "it" to point to the reason behind the latter statement. For example, instead of saying "Alice will go to Dinah because Alice wishes Dinah is with Alice", we could just say "Alice will go to Dinah because of it", since it is clear from the underlying context that the word "it" refers to the fact that Alice wants to be with Dinah.

#$
Alice wishes Dinah is with Alice.
Alice will go to Dinah because of it.
#$

<012>

It is also possible for us to join these two sentences into one, in which the two statements are separated by a comma and the word "so". In this alternative expression, it should be reasonable to say that the word "so" is a reference to the first of the two statements (i.e. "Alice wishes Dinah is with Alice"), whose job is to tell us why Alice is moving toward Dinah.

#$
Alice wishes Dinah is with Alice,
    so Alice will go to Dinah.
#$

<013>

@@<hr>
@@<h3><b>Belief</b></h3>

From an objective standpoint, however, it often turns out that a will alone does not qualify as the full proof of the validity of its subsequent action. In many cases, the desired consequence of an action is either unattainable due to an obstacle which cannot be removed, or it is already present (in which case the execution of the action would be pointless in the first place). Therefore, it must be sensible for us to say that a motive alone does not give us enough reason to carry out whichever act it requires; it is only when all the other prerequisites are met, that the motive will be imbued with enough power to claim its existence.

For instance, if we suppose that Dinah is already close to Alice, it will imply that Alice's wish is already fulfilled. In this case, therefore, she is not going to produce a meaningful result by attempting to move even closer to Dinah who is already close to her.

#$
Dinah is with Alice.
Alice wishes Dinah is with Alice.
#$

<014>

Does this mean that Alice is not even going to attempt to get closer to Dinah? Probably not. The fact that Alice wishes to be close to Dinah implies that Alice believes she is not close to Dinah yet.

#$
Dinah is with Alice.
Alice believes Dinah is not with Alice,
    so Alice wishes Dinah is with Alice.
#$

<015>

It is because Alice believes that Dinah is not with her, that Alice is justified in wishing to be close to Dinah. And because her wish is legitimate at least within the scope of her current belief system, Alice is bound to try being closer to Dinah whenever she meets an opportunity to do so.

#$
Dinah is with Alice.
Alice believes Dinah is not with Alice,
    so Alice wishes Dinah is with Alice,
    so Alice will go to Dinah.
#$

<016>

Something else is still missing, though. Why does Alice believe that Dinah is not with her? It is probably because Alice does not know that Dinah is with her already. Perhaps Dinah is out of Alice's sight, and it has been a while since Alice last saw Dinah. Whatever the reason, it is logical to conclude that Alice's belief in her isolation from Dinah is an inevitable consequence of her lack of awareness of the fact which says, "Dinah is with Alice".

#$
Dinah is with Alice.
Alice doesn't know Dinah is with Alice,
    so Alice believes Dinah is not with Alice,
    so Alice wishes Dinah is with Alice,
    so Alice will go to Dinah.
#$

<017>

There could be other necessary preconditions for Alice's action. Even if she wishes to be close to Dinah, for example, Alice won't attempt to make that happen if she is trapped inside a locked chamber. It is only when Alice has a goal AND there is an action she is able to execute for the purpose of bringing her closer to that goal, that she will be determined to execute it.

But before delving into this endless rabbit hole of details, let us focus more on the fundamental cause of the goal itself.

@@<hr>
@@<h3><b>Chain of Reasoning</b></h3>

In the preceding example, we saw that Alice has a desire to stay close to Dinah regardless of the circumstances. This means that "being close to Dinah" is Alice's ideal - a condition which contributes to Alice's overall satisfaction. As long as Alice believes that she is near Dinah, she will stay happy (unless there is a contrary factor which is bad enough to make her unhappy despite the presence of Dinah). Happiness is Alice's state of equilibrium.

If Alice is convinced that she is NOT near Dinah, then, it will signify a lost opportunity for Alice to maximize her happiness. This is when Alice realizes that, since being near to Dinah is her ideal and it is not achieved yet, she ought to wish for it and take measures to achieve it.

In order to understand how this flow of logic works internally, we need to think in terms of conditional statements. Doing this will enable us to justify the origin of each of Alice's decisions.

#$
IF Dinah is with Alice AND Alice doesn't know it,
    THEN Alice believes Dinah is not with Alice.
IF Alice believes Dinah is not with Alice,
    THEN Alice wishes Dinah is with Alice.
IF Alice wishes Dinah is with Alice,
    THEN Alice will go to Dinah.
#$

What is listed above are conditions and their corresponding results. One condition leads to another, which yet again leads to another and so forth, up until the point at which we are deprived of any more condition to satisfy and hence any newer fact to derive. Once all uncertainties are resolved and all knowable facts are known, our system will come to a halt and our story will be over.

A major problem with the conditionals above, though, is that they are way too specific and thus only applicable to our particular scenario which involves Alice, Dinah, and their relation of proximity. A more versatile approach would be to express them in a generic form by substituting each subject with a variable, like the ones shown below:

#$
IF Y is with X AND X doesn't know it,
    THEN X believes Y is not with X.
IF X believes Y is not with X,
    THEN X wishes Y is with X.
IF X wishes Y is with X,
    THEN X will go to Y.

X = Alice.
Y = Dinah.
#$

Yet this approach may still not be enough, and can potentially yield a huge number of redundant conditional expressions due to the fact that the rules we have established are only partially generic. As we encounter more and more ranges of scenarios, it is likely that we will end up dealing with conditions and their respective solutions which all happen to follow the same underlying pattern. Here is an example:

#$
IF Y is not hungry AND X doesn't know it,
    THEN X believes Y is hungry.
IF Y is not anxious AND X doesn't know it,
    THEN X believes Y is anxious.
IF Y is not bored AND X doesn't know it,
    THEN X believes Y is bored.
...
#$

Even with X and Y in place of Alice and Dinah, though, the repetition remains. For utmost simplicity, therefore, we need to abstract out the core elements of the conditionals as well.

#$
C is any condition which makes X happy.
S is any solution which makes C true.

IF C is true AND X doesn't know it,
    THEN X believes C is false.
IF X believes C is false,
    THEN X wishes C is true.
IF X wishes C is true,
    THEN X will execute S.

X = Alice.
Y = Dinah.
C = "Y is with X"
S = "Move X to Y"
#$

These are the generic rules of the story, from which a series of logical statements may unfold. Because of their abstract nature, we can easily imagine them as building blocks (e.g. gears and levers) of what I would call "a narrative engine" - a machine which takes a number of ingredients (e.g. Alice, Dinah, "is with") and brews a cup of fairy tale.

@@<hr>
@@<h3><b>Beyond Conditionals</b></h3>

I have introduced quite a number of narrative elements so far, at least from a logical point of view. A story begins when somebody carries out an action to pursue a goal, for it is motivation which awakens the world out of its mechanical slumber and drives its passion-generating engine.

What this is bound to yield is some kind of purposeful expectation - the underlying notion that one's actions, when aimed correctly and exercised sincerely, will eventually manifest themselves in a better future. What seems obvious at this point, then, is that the story will stop short if a single action is all it takes to produce the final result. In order to make the story more interesting, we need to come up with a long chain of actions and a long chain of intermediary goals. The hero's journey cannot be enriched with a plethora of emotional upheavals unless there are waves upon waves of conflicts, unexpected twists, and hidden layers of meaning. And in order to make room for these dynamic constituents, we need something more sophisticated than a mere list of objectives and their subsequent actions.

Our next volume will delve more deeply into this problem, and move on to introduce additional dimensions through which an unforeseen solution may emerge.

@@<h3><b>Progression</b></h3>

So far, I have introduced quite a number of ways in which a software engineer (or architect) could orchestrate the narrative aspect of the program by means of logical pieces. What is lacking, though, is a way to glue these fragmentary building blocks together and make them manifest themselves in an internally coherent and self-sustaining work of composition, which is capable of growing itself over time by chasing the complex trajectories of its surroundings.

What we need, in other words, is a method of establishing the idea of progression in whichever software product we happen to be designing. By following the course through which the application matures its own narrative, the user (i.e. master of the program) will be convinced that the content he/she is seeing is an elaborate self-expression of a meaningful substance, rather than a random heap of information which is irrelevant to his/her life.

@@<hr>
@@<h3><b>On the Fallacy of Arbitrary Rules</b></h3>

The most challenging side of such an objective is that, in order for a system to be endowed with its own mechanic of progression, a set of rules which are robust enough to give birth to a long self-growing chain of emergent behaviors is requisite. Yet as soon as we begin trying to come up with such rules, we find ourselves making up seemingly random logical assumptions which are quite out of place with the reality of our everyday lives.

For example, let us review the conditional statement below, which is one of the conditionals I introduced in the previous volume.

#$
IF Dinah is with Alice AND Alice doesn't know it,
    THEN Alice believes Dinah is not with Alice.
#$

What you are seeing here is essentially a rule which dictates that Alice must believe she is not near Dinah, as long as she is unaware of Dinah's nearby presence. An astute reader is likely to point out the obvious shortage of logical coherence in the foregoing conclusion and say, "It is absolute nonsense! Just because Alice doesn't know that Dinah is nearby does not mean that Alice is justified in believing that Dinah is far away from her!"

Before conceding that our example rule is downright flawed, of course, we should be justified in our claim that a belief is subjective in nature and therefore does not have to be logically sound. This argument, however, still does not tell us the reason why Alice would make such a choice of belief in the first place.

We could technically attribute Alice's decision to her personality as well as her personal memories, which would then play the role of the underlying causes behind her behavioral tendency. However, I am not yet going to conduct such an exhaustive survey of these contributing factors in order to fully unravel the origin of Alice's personal choices, for it is likely to be a painstaking task which will deter us from concentrating on the essence of the problem.

@@<hr>
@@<h3><b>A Sense of Purpose</b></h3>

A narrative begins with its own set of rules, for otherwise it will forever remain in its infantile toybox of sporadic daydreams. And even in the presence of fairly solid rules, the narrative quickly loses its emotional appeal as soon as the audience realizes that the rules themselves are contrived in an arbitrary manner without any shared objective.

It is necessary, therefore, to ensure that the rules are ultimately goal-driven, meaning that they ought to be designed to play their own parts in the manifestation of the "purpose of life". And in order to kickstart this monumental task, we first need to make sense of what "purpose of life" even means.

Whenever we say that the purpose of something is so-and-so, we are basically implying the existence of two distinct entities: (1) An ideal state which we haven't reached yet, and (2) An act which is supposed to bring us closer to that ideal state. A rule which is designed to make sense, then, will be a rule which invokes an act of directing the trajectory of the story toward the ideal we are aiming for. The question is, what exactly is that ideal we are trying to identify?

The main problem is that there seems to be an indefinitely long chain of ideals, which makes it rather difficult for us to reach its terminal point. Imagine, for instance, that Alice wants to be close to Dinah. If someone asks why, we may answer him/her by saying, "Because Alice is fond of seeing Dinah". If the person asks why Alice is fond of seeing Dinah, we may reply, "Because Dinah is Alice's cat and Alice has spent most of her childhood with Dinah, which makes her prefer seeing Dinah as often as possible in order to constantly bring up the sweet memories of her bygone days". And the person might question this as well, in which case we may respond by saying, "Because repeatedly reminding herself of her source of happiness is one of the most direct ways of maintaining her emotional stability, which reinforces Alice's confidence in her personal security". And, of course, the person may proceed to ask why personal security is considered important, in which case we will need to come up with a more fundamental reason to justify it, and so on.

As we start seeking the ultimate purpose of one's decision, we soon find ourselves being stuck in a ceaseless succession of inquiries. And apparently, there is no logical way to resolve this by simply pointing at something definite and saying, "THIS is the ultimate goal of life, the universe, and everything!" So, what shall we do to circumvent this apparent incompleteness in logic?

@@<hr>
@@<h3><b>Command</b></h3>

While the "ultimate goal" of the universe is not something we are able to fully identify right now (assuming that the very idea of purpose itself is still somewhat relevant on a cosmic scale), I would like to entertain the notion that it is of pragmatic value to suppose that there is some kind of supreme authority (i.e. God) which governs the full trajectory of our lives and dictates its destiny. Given this premise, we may anchor our intuition to the belief that the purpose of life is the fulfillment of commands which are handed to us by that authority, whose will is beyond our scope of rationality.

(Side Note: By means of the word "God", I do not mean to be speaking in any religious context. I am using this particular word just for the sake of indicating the hypothetical "administrator" of whichever system we happen to imagine.)

This, of course, could be considered a mere pretense which deliberately puts aside any chance of human reason by assuming the inevitable presence of a mystical black box. Yet it turns out to be a pretty useful stance to take, considering that it provides us with a solid starting point of wisdom - a set of axioms to which our focal point of sanity will be able to fall back even in times of the most devastating existential crisis.

Our next object of exploration should be the very definition of the word "command", for if we are to entertain the notion that a command is the most fundamental origin of our sense of purpose, we had better investigate the inner workings of the method by which the supreme authority assigns it to us and lets us be aware of its significance.

What do we mean by "command"? One thing I can tell with a high degree of confidence is that the existence of a command presumes the existence of an authority as well as its intention to exercise its power over a subject that is under control. Moreover, we can definitely say that a command always involves an objective (goal) which the authority expects its subject to pursue.

@@<hr>
@@<h3><b>Universal Law</b></h3>

If the subject of a command is universal (i.e. the command is addressed to everyone and not just to a specific individual), we can say that such a command is more of a "universal law" than a momentary task which only applies to a particular region in space and time. In English, this concept can be minimally expressed by means of a simple sentence, which may include verbs and objects but not a subject (because the subject is supposed to encompass the whole environment, which means an explicit mention of it is superfluous).

For example, a law which declares that Alice is a human being (rather than some other species) could be stated as the following:

#$
Let Alice be a human.
#$

<018>

Instead of just saying, "Alice is a human", the law above instructs the universe to put Alice into the category called "human", by means of the special keyword called "let" (which is what makes this sentence a command, rather than a mere announcement of an already established fact).

And the result of this is pretty straightforward. After the declaration of Alice as a human being (which is done by the command: "Let Alice be a human"), what we are expecting to see is a solidified fact which simply calls out the name of the class to which Alice belongs (i.e. "human").

#$
Let Alice be a human.
Alice is a human.
#$

<019>

@@<hr>
@@<h3><b>Cause and Effect</b></h3>

What we just witnessed above is a succession of two events, where the former is the cause of the latter. First of all, there is an establishment of a law (i.e. command) which tells us what "should be". Secondly, there is an establishment of a fact which tells us what "is", based on what the law says it should be.

At this point, you may wonder what exactly is the relation between these two events, and how to make sense of it grammatically. In the context of ordinary speech, it is probably going to make sense if we just say that the first statement (i.e. "Let Alice be a human") is the cause of the follow-up statement (i.e. "Alice is a human"). In this interpretation, the former is the cause of the latter and the latter is the effect of the former.

#$
Cause:
    Let Alice be a human.
Effect:
    Alice is a human.
#$

A more engineering-oriented interpretation would be to model these two statements as an input followed by its corresponding output. Suppose that the universe is a computer program and the user is its sole administrator who has the exclusive right to send commands to the engine of its inner cosmos. Each of these commands, then, will be a message that the program is going to evaluate for the purpose of generating its subsequent return value.

#$
Input:
    Let Alice be a human.
Output:
    Alice is a human.
#$

@@<hr>
@@<h3><b>Passage of Time</b></h3>

These foregoing depictions, though, are still not enough to let us lay down a firm conceptual foundation of what we are dealing with. The example scenario we saw is a transition in time - a modification of our universe from one state to another, each of which belongs to a distinct phase in time. Simply associating each statement with a descriptive word does not fully reflect the idea of change, which constitutes one of the core backbones of the very mechanic which impels the story to blossom over time.

For a quick demonstration of such a deficiency, let me revisit our previous example which is reproduced below.

#$
Let Alice be a human.
Alice is a human.
#$

<019>

Here, the main problem we notice is that there is no explicit differentiation between what belongs to the past, what belongs to the present, and what belongs to the future. The only hint we can find is that "Alice is a human" comes after "Let Alice be a human", which implicitly tells us that the act of letting Alice be defined as a human basically precedes the act of echoing the already declared fact that Alice indeed is a human.

Such an unspoken agreement may feel sufficient for now. As we start introducing more and more complex chains of causality to our system, however, the soundness of the assumption that the order of statements is a direct mirror image of the order in which causes and their effects are chained up sequentially is likely to fall apart, since it will be apparent to us that the concept of sequential order alone is incapable of fully representing scenarios in which multiple causes lead to a single effect, scenarios in which a single cause leads to multiple effects, or scenarios in which multiple causes simultaneously lead to multiple effects (aka "transaction").

It is necessary, therefore, to come up with a way to express temporal phenomena (e.g. causality) which is versatile enough to render a plethora of situations in an accurate manner, while also being fairly easy to understand.

First of all, we ought to understand what it really means to be in the past, present, or future. A short answer is that it depends on what the current time is. If the clock is showing us that it is 8:00AM, any event which happened before 8:00AM must belong to the past and any event which is bound to happen after 8:00AM must belong to the future. And this particular point in time (i.e. 8:00AM), which we commonly refer to as "now", is the division between past and future events which continuously shifts as time passes by.

In our everyday language, though, we usually do not denote such a temporal boundary in so precise a manner. Instead, when we find ourselves communicating with one another, we often just make use of tenses without mentioning exact moments in time, chiefly because such a level of detail is unnecessary in most cases.

In our example, therefore, it suffices to attach a temporal expression to each sentence to specify the phase in time to which it belongs. The following example illustrates how it can be done.

#$
God said, "Let Alice be a human".
Now Alice is a human.
#$

<020>

If we suppose that the existence of Alice as a human being (i.e. "Alice is a human") belongs to the present, it will be reasonable to claim that the preceding declaration of Alice as a human being (i.e. "Let Alice be a human") must have happened in the past.

Merely arranging the two events in the correct order, however, is still not enough. We should also state clearly that the latter was caused by the former in order to draw the full picture. Such a relation of causality can be expressed by joining the two statements with the word "so".

#$
God said, "Let Alice be a human",
    so now Alice is a human.
#$

<021>

And again, we may as well imagine the presence of a conditional statement (i.e. rule) through which these two statements express their logical coherence. For instance, if we do suppose that anything God says must come true right after he says it, we will be able to rephrase our foregoing expression in the following way:

#$
IF God said, "Let Alice be a human",
    THEN now Alice is a human.
#$

This, of course, is way too specific. We need a more general rule, which states that any definition of the form "Let X be Y" (where both X and Y can be anything), which is spoken by God, must be transformed into a fact which says, "X is Y".

#$
IF God said, "Let X be Y",
    THEN now X is Y.
#$

Still, this is not generic enough. A more comprehensive approach would be to set up a rule which allows God to make anything (i.e. X) true by simply saying it out loud and waiting for its effect to propagate through time.

#$
IF God said X,
    THEN now X.
#$

@@<hr>
@@<h3><b>Seed of Meaning</b></h3>

What you just saw is an example of a "fundamental law", which precedes all worldly truths because it touches the essence of pure mind without relying upon any other axiom. From this standpoint, we may proclaim that a sufficiently general law (e.g. IF God said X THEN now X) is able to function as a seed of meaning - i.e. the very fountain of inspiration from which innumerable branches of senses, feelings, and quantifiable patterns may emerge.

@@<h3><b>Emergence</b></h3>

In the previous volume, I explained the importance of having a sense of purpose in a software product, as well as the idea that a belief in the presence of a supreme authority, from which all goals ultimately originate, is necessary to enrich the software's semantic depth.

Our next question should be: "Okay, we are now supposing that there is some kind of 'God' who governs the system's own internal universe and endows it with a set of objectives. What are these objectives going to look like?"

Such a question, from an engineer's perspective, ought to be answered in terms of algorithms which are designed to exhibit emergent behaviors. A general philosophy that is shared across the mathematical sciences, including computer science, is that the highest form of elegance can be achieved when we let a minimal collection of logical building blocks (e.g. language patterns) be the author of a phantasmagoria of meaningful complexities, just like a seed is capable of bringing into existence the whole metabolic clockwork of a tree.

A powerful objective is one which is able to summon its own child-objectives (i.e. follow-up goals), each of which is almost as powerful as its parent. This notion can be imagined as what I would call "semantic recursion" - i.e. layers upon layers of self-referential intents which, as the story goes on, continually unfold themselves into ever-growing branches of volition.

Our next step in the process of designing a meaningful system, then, ought to involve the construction of goals which are able to reproduce themselves by means of recursion. In order to figure out how to do this, though, we first need to explore the very structure of our language and learn how to leverage it for our purposes.

@@<h3><b>Multiple Definitions</b></h3>

First off, let us revisit the premise we encountered before. It is a very simple one, which claims that anything God (i.e. the administrator of the system) said must come true right after the moment it was mentioned. Here, we are assuming that "X" stands for "anything".

#$
IF God said X,
    THEN now X.
#$

What we have not dealt with so far is the coexistence of multiple statements. God may say both X and Y, in which case the added complexity may force us to organize our grammar a bit more elegantly in order to keep things sufficiently clean. For instance, let us suppose that God made two separate proclamations; the first one declared that Alice must be a human being, and the second one declared that Dinah must be a cat.

#$
God said, "Let Alice be a human",
    so now Alice is a human.

God said, "Let Dinah be a cat",
    so now Dinah is a cat.
#$

<022>

In this scenario, there are two isolated causes followed by two isolated effects. These two chains of causality run in parallel, without necessarily interfering with each other. While both of them look pretty clean and innocent, one is still able to spot a considerable degree of syntactic redundancy here. What we inevitably end up learning from the above example is that these two independent statements, "Let Alice be a human" and "Let Dinah be a cat", are not required to be stated separately; instead, they can be bundled into a single sentence, thereby letting God say two things at once instead of one thing at a time. The only linguistic glue he needs to employ is the word "and", which is a magnet capable of inducing two separate bodies of meaning to attract each other and merge themselves into a single rigid body.

#$
God said, "Let Alice be a human
        and Dinah be a cat",
    so now Alice is a human
        and Dinah is a cat.
#$

<023>

Since the two causes are combined, their corresponding effects are also combined. Here, the word "and" may as well be imagined as a seed of divergence from which two parallel branches of events are destined to sprout.

It is also possible to multiply such branches indefinitely by means of "and". Let us suppose that, since humans and cats are both classified as animals, it is our desire to mention these facts rather explicitly. In this case, we could include two additional declarations as part of God's speech in order to cover a broader range of definitions (See the augmented expression below).

#$
God said, "Let Alice be a human
        and Dinah be a cat
        and human be animal
        and cat be animal",
    so now Alice is a human
        and Dinah is a cat
        and human is animal
        and cat is animal.
#$

@@<h3><b>Continuity</b></h3>

So far, we have been dealing with only two distinct phases in time: the past and the present. The past was when God said something, and the present is where its consequence has sprung into existence. What deserves our attention is the fact that such a consequence is not necessarily short-lived; it does not simply disappear into the haze of nothingness right after it was born, but instead maintains its own life as a true statement for the rest of eternity (as long as nothing comes along the way and makes it untrue at some point in time).

This means that if something is true now, it will also be true tomorrow, and the day after tomorrow, and the day after the day after tomorrow, and so on. If God said "Let Alice be a human" on Monday, for example, we will be assured that Alice will still remain as a human being on Tuesday unless God's words were somehow invalidated during the period of time between Monday and Tuesday.

#$
God said, "Let Alice be a human" on Monday,
    so Alice was a human on Tuesday.
#$

<024>

And there is no reason for such a definition to become falsified any time soon, since it is unlikely that God will change his mind and say, "Nah! Never mind, I would rather see Alice identifying herself as a potato". Therefore, if Alice was a human on Tuesday and nothing contrary happened meanwhile, she will still be a human on Wednesday. And if she was a human on Wednesday and nothing contrary happened meanwhile, she will still be a human on Thursday, and so forth.

#$
God said, "Let Alice be a human" on Monday,
    so Alice was a human on Tuesday,
    so Alice was a human on Wednesday,
    so Alice was a human on Thursday.
#$

<025>

Such a chain of truth statements is bound to keep growing and growing indefinitely as time goes on. Although I stopped right after Thursday in the above example (to save space), the persistence of the fact that Alice is a human being does not stop here. Since she was a human being on Thursday, she will keep being identified as a human being on Friday, Saturday, Sunday, the next week's Monday, the next week's Tuesday, etc.

There is an alternative way of expressing the foregoing scenario. Rather than traveling forward in time, we may as well be traveling backward in time by putting our clauses in the reverse order.

Here is how it works. We know that Alice was a human on Thursday. Why? Because she was a human on Wednesday. Why was she a human on Wednesday? Because she was a human on Tuesday. Why was she a human on Tuesday? Because God let her be a human on Monday.

#$
Alice was a human on Thursday,
    because Alice was a human on Wednesday,
    because Alice was a human on Tuesday,
    because God said, "Let Alice be a human" on Monday.
#$

<026>

Again, using the magic word "and" lets us mitigate a great deal of syntactic repetition by joining multiple things in one place. Instead of repeating the same clause of the form "Alice was a human on (...)" over and over again, we may as well just gather the list of days (i.e. Tuesday, Wednesday, Thursday) and put them all together by means of "and" (See the illustration below).

#$
God said, "Let Alice be a human" on Monday,
    so Alice was a human
        on Tuesday and Wednesday and Thursday.
#$

<027>

For a more thorough demonstration, let me come up with a richer example which makes extensive use of this special glue called "and".

If God made two statements (instead of just one statement) on Monday, we will be able to tell that these two will both claim their authenticity on Tuesday, Wednesday, Thursday, and so on. You can imagine them as two lines of truth which are running in parallel.

#$
God said, "Let Alice be a human
        and Dinah be a cat"
        on Monday,
    so Alice was a human
        and Dinah was a cat
        on Tuesday,
    so Alice was a human
        and Dinah was a cat
        on Wednesday,
    so Alice was a human
        and Dinah was a cat
        on Thursday.
#$

Here, we are observing a significant degree of repetition again. For sure, the word "and" is being used to combine the two definitions (i.e. "Alice was a human", "Dinah was a cat"), which helps us treat these two as a single unit. However, we could also leverage the word "and" to avoid repeating the same verbose pattern every time a day elapses.

#$
God said, "Let Alice be a human
        and Dinah be a cat"
        on Monday,
    so Alice was a human
        and Dinah was a cat
        on Tuesday and Wednesday and Thursday.
#$

@@<h3><b>Quality vs Quantity</b></h3>

What I haven't fully explained so far is the very mechanism which enables each God-spoken definition to linger over time. When God says, "Let Alice be a human", what makes us believe that Alice will indeed be a human and keep being defined as a human ever after? Why won't she just go back to her non-human state right after becoming a human?

In order to answer this question with an air of logical coherence, we've got to first notice the nature of temporal continuity. We normally say that time is continuous, due to the fact that individual moments in time can be arranged as a single sequence. Monday, Tuesday, Wednesday, and Thursday are all ordered in exactly one way and not in any other way. And this is precisely the reason why we have a consistent way of telling whether one moment in time comes before or after another.

Here is another way of putting it. We may claim that time is a quantity rather than a quality, since time is able to be expressed as a number. This is because we all know that a number is quantitative, in the sense that there is a fixed sequential order in which all numerical values are supposed to be arranged. A quality, on the other hand, is something which does not possess such an indisputably fixed order and thus cannot be represented by a number in a way which can be universally agreed upon (unless we all choose to quantify it by means of an arbitrary numerical representation - like that of an 'enum' data type).

So, for instance, we can easily imagine that the three elements of the game called "Rock Paper Scissors" (i.e. Rock, Paper, and Scissors) are qualities rather than quantities because there is no reason to assume that they are required to be ordered in exactly one way and never in some other way. One person may find it reasonable to arrange them as [Rock, Paper, Scissors], while another person may find it more reasonable to arrange them as [Scissors, Paper, Rock]; and both arrangements are equally valid, as long as we are not attaching some kind of cultural preference to our faculty of judgement.

<028>

Since time is quantitative, we know that there is exactly one way in which our days are supposed to be arranged. And I am pretty sure that everyone will agree on this; we all know that Monday precedes (i.e. comes before) Tuesday, Tuesday precedes Wednesday, and Wednesday precedes Thursday.

#$
Monday precedes Tuesday.
Tuesday precedes Wednesday.
Wednesday precedes Thursday.
#$

<029>

What you can clearly notice here is a linear chain of events (i.e. a succession of days), each of which carries its own logic over to the next one in the sequence. This is reminiscent of induction, whose proposition begins at some base case and recursively replicates itself to infinity like a radio wave. This pattern could be generalized in the following way:

When God says something (i.e. X) on a particular day, it should be logical for us to conclude that this "something" will become a true statement tomorrow (i.e. the next day) as long as our supposition is that it takes one day for God's words to be fully realized.

#$
IF God said X on Day1
        AND Day1 precedes Day2,
    THEN X on Day2.
#$

And the other general rule is that if this "something" has been true today, it will stay true tomorrow. Such an underlying assumption, of course, may need to be adjusted if we decide to take into account the possibility of some unexpected event disrupting the usual state of affairs. But for the sake of simplicity, let us imagine for the time being that a true statement will continue being true.

#$
IF X on Day1
        AND Day1 precedes Day2,
    THEN X on Day2.
#$

@@<h3><b>Potential Causes of Discontinuity</b></h3>

As the apparent logical fallacy of the above rule tells us, it is our responsibility to recognize the complexity of temporal phenomena and take measures to reduce it to a form which is structurally minimal yet still flexible enough to handle a wide range of edge cases. A rough sketch of such a line of reasoning could be expressed in the following way:

#$
IF X on Day1
        AND Day1 precedes Day2
        AND "Nothing interrupts X on Day1",
    THEN X on Day2.
#$

Here, the verdict is that even if X is true, it will persist only as long as nothing comes along the way and interrupts its state of truth. The problem of identifying factors which contribute to such interruption is not a trivial matter, and thus it will be explored in the upcoming volumes.

The possibility of disturbance in the state of equilibrium during the absence of God's micromanagement is perhaps the most rudimentary source of chaos. It segments the system into a group of individual minds which, due to their mutually conflicting desires, frequently shift the focal point of our collective worldview in unexpected ways.

@@<h3><b>From Definition to Purpose</b></h3>

What has hitherto been illustrated is not the end of the story, of course. Definitions such as "Alice is a human" do not constitute the core engine of the narrative. To be able to devise a storyline which is "meaningful" in some way or another, one needs to convince the audience that something is not in its ideal state yet, so that we have a moral obligation to change it into a form which matches the ideal more closely. The question of what we mean by "ideal" requires us to raise a solid wall of distinction between things that are "good" and things that are "bad". The reason behind this is that our sense of purpose is greatly influenced by the notion that the way we fulfill it is by maximizing the number of good things while minimizing the number of bad things in the world.
